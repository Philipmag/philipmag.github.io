## 1. How to use this guide

This is the operating manual for the six student employees who serve as Senior Facilitators. It explains how to prepare, deliver, coordinate, observe, and improve the full eight-week program for 50 to 60 student participants. It is written so that a newly hired Senior Facilitator can understand the sequence of work, the expected evidence of learning, and the limits of their authority.

This guide turns the proposal into a workable delivery model. Where the proposal identifies an item as still under development—such as the final assessment rubric, host-site schedule, or supervision arrangement—this guide labels the process **proposed**. The Program Coordinator / Academic Lead must confirm or replace those processes before delivery. This manual never overrides Trent University academic, accessibility, privacy, employment, health-and-safety, community-partner, or records-management requirements.

## 2. Program overview and context

Digital Guardians is an eight-week, community-engaged micro-credential. It prepares student participants to help older adults use technology safely, confidently, independently, and with dignity. Participants learn about common scams, phishing, impersonation, fraud, password and privacy practices, safe browsing, video communication, and introductory artificial-intelligence literacy. They also learn how to explain technology in plain language, create accessible learning activities, listen carefully, adapt to different confidence levels, and facilitate respectfully with older adults.

The program serves two groups. The primary learners are 50 to 60 student participants who work in teams of four to six. The community beneficiaries are older adults at approved partners such as Activity Haven, Peterborough Public Library, the New Canadians Centre, or another approved organization that serves older adults. These named sites are prospective partners, not confirmed placements. No workshop date, participant assignment, or service commitment is final until the Program Coordinator / Academic Lead and host partner approve it.

The program is intentionally more than a cybersecurity-information course. A participant must demonstrate that they can understand a digital-safety issue, explain it clearly, apply it responsibly, support another person respectfully, and reflect on how to improve. This is why community-engaged practice, scenario work, reflection, current-threat research, and the capstone portfolio are central rather than optional additions.

## 4. What competency-based means in practice

Competency-based assessment asks whether a participant can **demonstrate** the required capability. In this program, a participant does not complete the micro-credential merely by attending a workshop, submitting a document, or repeating a definition. They must show safe and accurate digital knowledge, plain-language communication, respectful facilitation, sound judgment, and reflection that leads to improvement.

For Senior Facilitators, this changes how feedback is given. Do not write only “good work” or “needs improvement.” Point to observable evidence. For example: “Your explanation correctly identified an unexpected password-reset request as suspicious and told the learner not to click the link. Before the community session, add one sentence explaining how to independently reach the institution through a trusted website or phone number.” This feedback names what worked, identifies a concrete gap, and gives the participant a next step.

The proposal provides one numerical standard: participants are expected to complete online modules and knowledge checks with a proposed minimum of **80%**. The proposal does not assign weighted percentages to the remaining evidence. Until formal approval, use the following **proposed competency rule**: all required evidence must be completed; safety-critical concerns must be corrected; and the Academic Lead or designated assessor must determine whether the evidence demonstrates the required standard. Do not invent a final mark by averaging informal scores.

Do not divide work so rigidly that a workshop or feedback task depends on a single unavailable employee.

## 6. Delivery principles for every session

### 6.1 Teach with dignity

Older adults are not a problem to be fixed and are not a single, uniform group. Participants must not use infantilizing language, rush people, assume lack of experience, or take over devices without consent. Model the language you expect: “Would you like to try that step while I talk it through?” is better than “Give it to me; I’ll do it.”

### 6.2 Use plain language and short steps

Introduce one concept at a time. Use a brief definition, a realistic example, the safe action, and a question that checks understanding. Avoid long lectures, unexplained acronyms, and fear-based warnings. For a technical term such as multi-factor authentication, first explain its purpose: “It adds a second check so another person cannot get into your account with only your password.”

### 6.3 Keep safety and privacy visible

Never ask anyone to reveal passwords, verification codes, banking information, government identification numbers, or private messages that are not necessary for the learning example. Do not click an unknown link, install an unverified app, contact a suspected scammer, access a participant’s account, or provide financial, legal, medical, or law-enforcement advice.

### 6.4 Teach transferable judgment, not only a button sequence

A useful activity asks participants to notice an unexpected request, pause, check a trusted source, avoid acting under pressure, and seek help if unsure. The exact location of a setting can change between devices; the habit of slowing down and verifying remains useful.

### 6.5 Make practice visible

Every learning session must include a chance for student participants to explain, demonstrate, rehearse, or revise. Senior Facilitators should observe those moments and use them as evidence for coaching. Community readiness grows through repeated practice, not through slides alone.

## 7. Standard weekly operating rhythm

The participant schedule uses six hours per week. Senior Facilitators prepare and support each block. Exact days and rooms are determined locally, but the educational sequence should remain stable.

|---|---:|---|---|

| Community workshop participation or facilitation | 1 hour | Arrange approved partner contact, observation, or structured community-practice preparation. | Attendance/participation record and observation notes where applicable. |

If an approved community site offers an earlier observation or low-risk participation opportunity, the Program Coordinator / Academic Lead may authorize it. Otherwise, use the weekly community-practice block for partner orientation, workshop set-up, observation of approved programming, rehearsal, or site-specific preparation. Do not claim that a participant has completed live facilitation until the required supervised community-workshop evidence has been recorded.

## 9. Week-by-week delivery plan

The following plan preserves the proposal’s learning progression. The proposed due dates for the four threat briefs are Weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8. These dates may be adjusted by the Academic Lead, but four briefs, eight reflections, a workshop plan, observed facilitation, and a capstone portfolio remain required evidence under the current proposal.

### Week 1 — Digital inclusion, dignity, and the landscape of online risk

**Learning goal.** Participants understand the purpose of Digital Guardians, the importance of digital inclusion, and the difference between helping someone use technology and taking control away from them. They begin to recognize common online risks: phishing, impersonation, fraud, suspicious links, social engineering, malware, misinformation, and unexpected requests.

**Senior Facilitator preparation.** The Learning and Curriculum Lead prepares a plain-language introduction and short scenarios. The Student Mentorship Lead finalizes team assignments and check-in contacts. The Digital Safety Lead validates scenarios against approved sources. The Accessibility Lead checks that materials are readable and that all participants can access the learning platform. The Assessment Lead opens the evidence tracker.

**How to run the week.** In the applied-practice session, use paired role plays. One participant explains a suspicious text to a peer who asks basic questions. The observer uses a short checklist: Did the facilitator avoid jargon? Did they name the risky cue? Did they suggest a safe next step? Rotate roles so every participant practises speaking and listening.

**Community-practice block.** If no approved site activity is scheduled, use one hour for partner orientation, reviewing a fictional older-adult learner profile, and practising a respectful welcome. Never use stereotypes in profiles. Describe a learner’s goal, device context, and comfort level rather than their assumed ability.

**Evidence and feedback.** Record module completion, Knowledge Check 1, Reflection 1, team contact plan, and initial practice observations. Give each team one strength and one improvement priority during the debrief.

**Materials.** Program map; community-conduct guide; scenario cards; plain-language phrase bank; practice-observation checklist; assessment checklist; reflection prompt.

### Week 2 — Scam recognition, phishing, impersonation, and fraud response

**Learning goal.** Participants can recognize common indicators of phishing, impersonation, fraud, account compromise, suspicious links, urgency pressure, and requests for personal information. They can explain the safe response in clear, non-alarming language.

**Senior Facilitator preparation.** The Digital Safety Lead prepares current, credible examples stripped of personal data. The Learning and Curriculum Lead prepares a “pause, verify, and seek help” explanation framework. The Assessment Lead releases the first threat-brief template and checks that participants understand its required elements.

**How to run the week.** Use a scenario carousel. Teams review email, text, phone-call, social-media, and AI-enabled impersonation examples. For each example, they identify the cue, explain why it matters, write a plain-language response, and identify a safe verification path. The Senior Facilitators must correct unsafe advice immediately and explain why it is unsafe.

**Community-practice block.** Where authorized, participants observe or prepare a short five-minute “spotting suspicious requests” mini-lesson. They must not ask older adult attendees to open messages or enter credentials.

**Evidence and feedback.** Collect Knowledge Check 2, Threat Brief 1, and Reflection 2. Threat Brief 1 should identify a current or emerging risk, cite credible source material, explain relevance to older adults, and provide safe, plain-language guidance. Senior Facilitators give formative feedback; the approved academic assessor retains formal assessment authority.

**Materials.** Current-threat brief template; source-evaluation guide; scam scenario cards; safe-verification flow; feedback rubric; observation checklist.

### Week 3 — Passphrases, password managers, and multi-factor authentication

**Learning goal.** Participants can explain strong passphrases, the value of unique passwords, the purpose of password managers, and how multi-factor authentication adds protection. They can demonstrate without requesting real credentials.

**Senior Facilitator preparation.** The Learning and Curriculum Lead prepares a fictional account demonstration. The Accessibility Lead verifies that the demonstration can be viewed clearly and has a no-internet backup. The Digital Safety Lead confirms that the explanation does not recommend sharing or recording actual passwords.

**How to run the week.** Begin with a misconception check: “A complicated password used everywhere is safer than a unique passphrase for each account.” Guide participants to explain why reuse creates risk. Then use a fictional account to demonstrate account protection. Participants practise describing multi-factor authentication to a learner who worries they will be locked out.

**Community-practice block.** Teams rehearse a 10-minute password-safety mini-lesson and receive peer feedback. If an approved community session occurs, Senior Facilitators observe and document only the facilitator’s practice—not community members’ personal account details.

**Evidence and feedback.** Record Knowledge Check 3 and Reflection 3. Review Threat Brief 1 feedback during the team debrief. Teams begin a draft workshop plan that identifies an objective, a plain-language explanation, a demonstration, a practice task, and safe boundaries.

**Materials.** Fictional-account script; passphrase examples that are not real passwords; multi-factor authentication visual; workshop-plan template; peer-feedback prompt.

### Week 4 — Privacy, safe browsing, updates, and account/device security

**Learning goal.** Participants can explain privacy settings, safe browsing, software updates, account protection, and device-security habits. They can connect a safety recommendation to a practical reason without making promises that technology cannot guarantee.

**Senior Facilitator preparation.** The Digital Safety Lead reviews examples for currency. The Accessibility Lead prepares screenshots or device-neutral guides. The Assessment Lead schedules Threat Brief 2 review and confirms which workshop-plan elements must be complete before Week 6.

**How to run the week.** Use a “safe choice or risky choice” activity based on realistic browser, privacy, update, and account-recovery decisions. Then ask each team to teach a three-step safety action to another team. Observers note clarity, pacing, accuracy, and whether the facilitator checks understanding.

**Community-practice block.** Approved partner orientation, site observation, or a structured rehearsal can focus on how to introduce privacy without making older adults feel blamed. The Community Workshop Operations Lead confirms any future site needs.

**Evidence and feedback.** Collect Knowledge Check 4, Threat Brief 2, Reflection 4, and a workshop-plan draft. Give written feedback on the draft plan before Week 5 so teams have time to revise. Flag any unsafe advice, unclear learning objective, inaccessible wording, or missing practice activity.

**Materials.** Safe-browsing scenarios; privacy plain-language guide; update and account-protection checklists; workshop-plan rubric; threat-brief template.

### Week 5 — Safe video communication and accessible digital connection

**Learning goal.** Participants can help older adults use video-conferencing and communication tools safely. They can explain how to join a call, use basic privacy controls, recognize suspicious invitations, and avoid unnecessary sharing.

**Senior Facilitator preparation.** The Accessibility Lead tests the approved communication tool, device connections, audio, captions where available, and a backup method. The Learning and Curriculum Lead prepares a short, device-neutral walkthrough. The Community Workshop Operations Lead confirms whether host sites have equipment or access limitations.

**How to run the week.** Start with a live but fictional meeting invitation. Participants identify information that should be checked before joining. Then run a guided practice in which each team takes turns helping a learner join a mock call, mute, adjust camera use, and leave safely. Emphasize consent before changing any setting.

**Community-practice block.** Teams conduct or rehearse a small group communication activity. Senior Facilitators watch for over-explaining, taking over a device, or failing to notice accessibility needs. Correct these issues in private coaching.

**Evidence and feedback.** Record Knowledge Check 5 and Reflection 5. Teams revise workshop plans and teaching materials based on Week 4 feedback. The Assessment Lead verifies that all teams have a named topic, objective, activity, safety boundary, accessibility plan, and materials list.

**Materials.** Mock meeting invitations; video-call quick guide; privacy-control checklist; accessible-demonstration checklist; revised workshop-plan template.

### Week 6 — Introductory AI literacy, privacy, limitations, and verification

**Learning goal.** Participants can use and explain introductory AI tools responsibly. They understand safe prompting, everyday uses, limits, privacy concerns, misinformation risks, and the need to verify important AI-generated information.

**Senior Facilitator preparation.** The Digital Safety Lead prepares approved, non-sensitive examples and a verification exercise. The Accessibility Lead confirms that a low-tech alternative is available if an AI tool is inaccessible or unavailable. The Learning and Curriculum Lead prepares a simple explanation that does not overstate AI reliability.

**How to run the week.** Demonstrate a harmless everyday prompt using no personal information. Compare an AI answer with a credible source and ask participants to identify uncertainty or an error. Then have teams practise explaining: “AI can be helpful, but it can be wrong. Do not enter private information, and check important answers with a trusted source.”

**Community-practice block.** Teams rehearse the final version of their workshop with another team acting as older adult learners. Senior Facilitators conduct a readiness check using the proposed observation rubric and require revisions for safety-critical issues.

**Evidence and feedback.** Collect Knowledge Check 6, Threat Brief 3, Reflection 6, and final workshop plan/materials. Do not clear a team for community delivery until the Program Coordinator / Academic Lead or delegated reviewer confirms the approved readiness process. The assessment tracker should identify any participant who is behind on modules, briefs, reflections, or preparation.

**Materials.** AI use-and-limits guide; verification exercise; AI privacy prompt card; final workshop-plan checklist; readiness observation form; threat-brief template.

### Week 7 — Supervised community workshop: delivery cycle one

**Learning goal.** Participants apply their learning in a supervised community setting. They demonstrate accuracy, patience, communication, safety, accessibility, responsiveness, teamwork, and willingness to adapt.

**Senior Facilitator preparation.** The Community Workshop Operations Lead confirms the approved site run sheet. The Accessibility Lead prepares devices and backup materials. The Assessment Lead assigns an observer and secure observation form to each participant team. The Student Mentorship Lead completes a short pre-session check-in with each team. The Digital Safety Lead reviews any scenario content that may have changed.

**How to run the week.** Each participant team arrives early, sets up with permission, welcomes attendees, states the session purpose, and invites questions. Senior Facilitators remain visible and supportive but avoid taking over. If a participant shares an apparent scam, the facilitator should slow down, prevent risky action where possible, and use the approved escalation route rather than attempting a personal case investigation.

**Observation and feedback.** The assigned observer records only observable behaviour. During or immediately after the session, provide a short private debrief: one demonstrated strength, one priority change, and one action for the next workshop. Preserve participant dignity; do not compare teams publicly.

**Evidence and feedback.** Record workshop attendance and participation, observed-facilitation evidence, Reflection 7, team debrief notes, and portfolio artefacts such as the approved plan, revised handout, and feedback summary. Identify any missing requirement immediately so the participant has a supported opportunity to address it in Week 8.

**Materials.** Site run sheet; attendance record; observer rubric; safe-escalation card; workshop plan; approved handouts; materials checklist; debrief form; incident/escalation record where needed.

### Week 8 — Supervised community workshop: delivery cycle two and portfolio completion

**Learning goal.** Participants demonstrate growth by applying feedback in a second supervised workshop and consolidating evidence in a final portfolio and reflection.

**Senior Facilitator preparation.** Review Week 7 observations, prepare targeted coaching, confirm the second workshop schedule, and ensure that each participant knows which evidence remains incomplete. The Assessment Lead prepares the portfolio checklist and status report for the Academic Lead.

**How to run the week.** Start with a focused team rehearsal based on Week 7 feedback. During the workshop, observe whether the participant has addressed the identified priority. After the workshop, facilitate a structured closing debrief that asks: What did you explain well? What did the older adult participants appear to understand? What would you change next time? What evidence belongs in your portfolio?

**Evidence and feedback.** Collect Threat Brief 4, Reflection 8, end-of-program reflection, final workshop evidence, and capstone portfolio. The Senior Facilitators check completeness and provide final formative feedback. The Academic Lead or designated assessor reviews the complete evidence and makes the final badge decision.

**Materials.** Final observer rubric; portfolio checklist; end-of-program reflection prompt; threat-brief template; completion-status tracker; participant feedback summary form; final debrief guide.

## 10. Mentorship and support process

### 10.1 Cohort assignment and contact rhythm

At onboarding, assign each participant a named Senior Facilitator mentor. The mentor is the first contact for routine questions about deadlines, team communication, rehearsal, feedback interpretation, and workshop preparation. Assign each team a second Senior Facilitator contact for coverage. Share only approved professional contact methods and response expectations.

Use a minimum weekly contact rhythm. The mentor reviews the team’s status before the debrief, meets the team briefly during or after the debrief, and records one agreed next step. The contact may be a 10- to 15-minute check-in, a structured group conversation, or a documented review of a submitted item. Use larger group sessions for common questions and private conversations for individual concerns.

### 10.2 Coaching method

Use the following four-step coaching sequence.

1. **Describe the evidence.** State what was observed without judging the person. Example: “Your demonstration included six instructions before the learner could practise.”
2. **Connect it to the competency.** Explain why it matters. Example: “Age-friendly facilitation uses manageable steps and checks understanding.”
3. **Ask for the participant’s interpretation.** Example: “Where do you think the learner might have lost track?”
4. **Agree on one next action.** Example: “For the next practice, pause after each two steps and invite the learner to try the action.”

Do not use mentorship meetings to make informal final grading decisions. If feedback identifies a potential safety, academic-integrity, accessibility, conduct, or serious participation issue, document the facts and escalate through the approved route.

### 10.3 Supporting teams that are struggling

When a team misses a requirement or struggles with preparation, identify the barrier early. A team may need a revised schedule, a content refresher, a practice run, clearer division of roles, accessible materials, or a discussion with the Academic Lead. The Senior Facilitator should record the support offered and follow up on the agreed action.

If a team conflict persists, use a structured conversation: define the task, identify the specific disruption, ask each person to describe needed changes, document an agreed plan, and escalate if the issue continues or involves conduct concerns. Do not ask Senior Facilitators to investigate allegations beyond their authority.

### 11.2 The current-threat brief template

Each brief should be short and focused. Unless the Academic Lead provides a different template, use the following structure.

| Required element | What the participant must show |
|---|---|
| Threat or issue | A clear title and short description of a current scam, cyber risk, or emerging technology issue. |
| Credible source base | At least one approved credible public source, cited according to the program’s approved practice. |
| Relevance to older adults | A specific explanation of why the issue may affect older adults or community workshop participants. |
| Risk cues | The observable warning signs, without teaching unsafe actions. |
| Safe next steps | Clear, non-alarming actions such as pause, avoid clicking, verify through a trusted route, and seek appropriate help. |
| Plain-language translation | A short explanation suitable for an older adult audience. |
| Reflection | What the participant would emphasize or avoid when facilitating this topic. |

Senior Facilitators should not approve poor-quality sources simply because a brief is submitted on time. If a source is unreliable, explain the concern and direct the participant to approved research guidance.

### 11.3 Weekly reflection prompt

Each participant submits one reflection per week. The prompt should be adjusted to the week’s activity but should always ask the learner to identify what they learned, where their current understanding is incomplete, how they used or will use feedback, and what they will do next.

A usable standard prompt is: “Describe one concept or facilitation skill you understand better this week. Give one example of how you practised it. Identify one question, uncertainty, or improvement need. State one concrete action you will take before the next session.”

### 11.4 Workshop-plan review process

A workshop plan must be reviewed before community delivery. Use the following pre-delivery checklist.

| Required plan element | Review question |
|---|---|
| Learning objective | Can an older adult participant reasonably understand and practise this objective in the available time? |
| Core message | Is the explanation accurate, plain-language, and free of unnecessary jargon? |
| Demonstration | Does it use a fictional or safe example and avoid real credentials or private information? |
| Hands-on practice | Does the plan let older adult participants try a manageable step if they choose? |
| Safety boundary | Does the plan state what facilitators will not do, such as access accounts or handle financial credentials? |
| Accessibility | Are materials readable, paced, device-aware, and adaptable to different needs? |
| Question time | Does the schedule allow time for questions, repetition, and respectful clarification? |
| Escalation route | Does the team know whom to contact if a safety, privacy, or site issue occurs? |

The Learning and Curriculum Lead, Accessibility Lead, Digital Safety Lead, and Assessment Lead should coordinate the review. A team must revise unsafe or inaccessible elements before a community workshop. The Program Coordinator / Academic Lead confirms the final approval process.

### 11.5 Proposed observation rubric for live facilitation

The proposal names six criteria: **accuracy, patience, communication, safety, accessibility, and responsiveness**. Until a formally approved rubric is issued, use the following proposed observation guide for formative feedback. It is not a substitute for the official academic rubric.

| Criterion | Evidence of developing practice | Evidence of competent practice | Safety-critical concern requiring immediate correction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | Gives a mostly correct explanation but needs support to clarify details. | Gives correct, current, and appropriately qualified information; admits uncertainty and checks approved sources when needed. | Gives inaccurate advice that could expose a person to fraud, privacy loss, or account compromise. |
| Patience | Allows some practice but may rush, interrupt, or move on too quickly. | Uses a calm pace, invites questions, repeats steps when helpful, and respects learner choice. | Uses shaming, dismissive, or coercive behaviour. |
| Communication | Provides information but uses unexplained jargon or overly long directions. | Uses plain language, short steps, examples, and check-for-understanding questions. | Gives directions that are incomprehensible or pressures a learner to disclose private information. |
| Safety | Names some safe habits but needs reminders about boundaries. | Models pause-and-verify habits, protects privacy, and follows escalation procedures. | Requests or handles passwords, verification codes, banking credentials, or private account access; clicks suspicious links; ignores an immediate safety risk. |
| Accessibility | Uses materials that are partly usable but does not consistently adapt pace or format. | Uses readable materials, clear pacing, hands-on practice, consent-based support, and appropriate adaptation. | Excludes, humiliates, or disregards an identified accessibility need. |
| Responsiveness | Notices some questions but needs help adapting the activity. | Listens carefully, responds within role boundaries, adapts explanation, and refers issues appropriately. | Ignores a disclosure or concern that must be escalated under approved procedures. |

The observer records brief factual examples, not personality judgments. A concise record might state: “Explained why the link was suspicious; asked the attendee to pause and independently find the bank’s official number; allowed the attendee to try the next step; used a large-print handout.”

## 12. Facilitating community workshops with older adults

### 12.1 Before the workshop

The Community Workshop Operations Lead creates a site run sheet at least several working days before the session, subject to partner timelines. It must identify the site contact, arrival time, room, expected participant range, equipment, accessibility notes communicated through approved channels, workshop objective, participant-team assignment, assigned Senior Facilitator observer, materials list, and escalation contacts.

The participant team must complete a readiness review. They must be able to state the learning objective, explain the topic in plain language, demonstrate a safe example, invite hands-on practice, protect privacy, and describe what they will do if a participant asks for help outside their role. If a team cannot meet these requirements, arrange additional practice and consult the Program Coordinator / Academic Lead before community delivery.

### 12.2 During the workshop

Begin with a welcome and a simple statement of purpose. Explain that questions are welcome and that no one needs to share passwords, financial information, or other private details. Set a calm pace. Demonstrate one step at a time and invite participants to try it on their own device only if they wish.

Senior Facilitators should use a coverage model: one observes each participant team; one acts as site-day operations contact; one is available for safe technical or accessibility support; and the others circulate or provide relief. Adjust the arrangement to the number of teams and approved site conditions. An observer must not create a distracting second lesson while a participant team is leading.

If an older adult attendee shows a possible scam, do not click, reply, download, call a number in the message, or enter information. Help the person pause. Explain the relevant general safety principle. Refer them to an approved trusted route or supervisor according to the program’s escalation process. Do not access their accounts or make a decision on their behalf.

### 12.3 After the workshop

Run a short private debrief with the participant team while the evidence is fresh. Ask them what they noticed, what appeared to help older adult attendees learn, what question was difficult, and what they would change. The observer shares factual feedback using the approved rubric. The Assessment Lead records the evidence status; the Student Mentorship Lead follows up with any participant who needs extra support.

Only collect community feedback through approved methods. Summarize patterns without recording unnecessary personal information about older adult attendees.

## 13. Coordination among the six Senior Facilitators

### 13.1 Meeting cadence

Use the following default cadence unless the Program Coordinator / Academic Lead sets another schedule.

| Meeting | Recommended timing | Purpose | Required record |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly planning meeting | Before the new learning week; 45–60 minutes | Confirm content, evidence deadlines, team needs, materials, partner logistics, and risk changes. | Action list with owner and due date. |
| Short site-day huddle | Before each community workshop; 15–20 minutes | Confirm roles, site run sheet, safety boundaries, and escalation contacts. | Updated site run sheet. |
| Weekly debrief | After community activity; 30–45 minutes | Review observations, recurring questions, missing evidence, partner feedback, and changes for next week. | Confidential team debrief notes. |
| Academic Lead check-in | Weekly or as directed | Escalate academic, safety, conduct, accessibility, partner, or operational issues. | Decision and follow-up record. |

### 13.2 Decision-making rule

Use the “owner decides, team informs” rule for routine operations. The person with the relevant portfolio proposes and completes the task after checking affected colleagues. For example, the Community Workshop Operations Lead owns the site run sheet, while the Accessibility Lead confirms material and device readiness.

Decisions that affect academic assessment, student safety, privacy, accessibility commitments, host-site commitments, public messaging, allegations, budget, or program scope must be referred to the Program Coordinator / Academic Lead or the appropriate university authority. Senior Facilitators must not make unilateral commitments in these areas.

### 13.3 Escalation paths

| Concern | Immediate Senior Facilitator response | Escalate to |
|---|---|---|
| Possible fraud, account compromise, or unsafe digital action | Pause risky action; do not access accounts; provide only approved general safety guidance. | Program Coordinator / Academic Lead and approved site process. |
| Privacy or sensitive-information exposure | Stop unnecessary collection or sharing; preserve confidentiality. | Program Coordinator / Academic Lead and required university privacy route. |
| Accessibility barrier | Identify the barrier, use an available approved adaptation, and document the need. | Program Coordinator / Academic Lead and accessibility process. |
| Participant conduct, conflict, or harassment concern | Maintain safety, document observable facts, avoid investigation beyond role. | Program Coordinator / Academic Lead and applicable university process. |
| Academic integrity or missing assessment evidence | Record facts, offer routine support if appropriate, avoid final judgement. | Academic Lead or designated assessor. |
| Community-partner concern or site disruption | Follow site instructions, protect attendees, and avoid unapproved promises. | Community Workshop Operations Lead and Program Coordinator / Academic Lead. |
| Emergency or immediate threat to safety | Follow host-site emergency procedures and call emergency services where required. | Site contact and Program Coordinator / Academic Lead as soon as safe. |

## 14. Materials and resource control

The Accessibility, Technology and Resources Lead maintains a shared, approved resource library. The library should contain only current, reviewed materials. Use clear filenames, dates, and version numbers. Remove or archive outdated examples so a participant does not accidentally teach a superseded scam pattern or incorrect device instruction.

### Weekly resource pack

Before each week, publish a resource pack containing the approved agenda, module links, slides or facilitator script, scenario cards, relevant handouts, activity instructions, reflection prompt, threat-brief template when due, assessment checklist, and escalation contacts. For any community workshop, add the site run sheet, final workshop plan, attendance/observation forms, materials checklist, and backup plan.

## 15. Data handling and record discipline

Use only the university-approved platform or storage location for participant assessment records. Limit access to the people who need it for program delivery or assessment. Do not store sensitive information in informal chat messages, personal devices, public documents, or workshop handouts.

Observation notes should describe facilitation behaviour, not make personal or speculative judgments. Community feedback should be aggregated where practical. Never include an older adult attendee’s passwords, account details, banking information, identification numbers, health information, or other unnecessary personal data in program records.

## 16. Completion close-out

At the end of Week 8, the Assessment and Feedback Lead prepares a completion-status report for the Academic Lead. The report identifies evidence received, feedback provided, outstanding requirements, and any safety-critical issues that remain unresolved. It does not award the badge.

The Academic Lead or designated assessor reviews the evidence and makes the final decision according to the approved micro-credential process. Senior Facilitators then complete any approved communication, archive records according to policy, thank community partners through the appropriate channel, and record operational improvements for the next delivery.

## Appendix A — Facilitator pre-session checklist

Before every participant learning session, confirm the following.

- The learning objective matches the week’s sequence and approved content.
- The agenda includes explanation, demonstration, practice, and debrief.
- Examples are accurate, current, non-sensational, and free of personal data.
- Materials use plain language and accessible formatting.
- The assigned Senior Facilitators know their roles and cohort follow-up tasks.
- The assessment artefact for the week is available and its due date is clear.
- The escalation contact and safe-facilitation boundary are visible.
- Technology and a backup activity are ready.

## Appendix B — Facilitator community workshop checklist

Before leaving for an approved site, confirm the following.

- The site run sheet, room, arrival time, contact, and workshop objective are confirmed.
- Participant teams have an approved plan and materials.
- Every team has a named Senior Facilitator observer or support contact.
- Accessible-format materials, devices, chargers, and backup materials are packed.
- Attendance and observation forms are available in the approved format.
- The facilitation team knows not to request credentials, access accounts, or click suspicious links.
- Emergency, privacy, accessibility, and partner escalation paths are available.
- The team has planned a private debrief and evidence hand-off after the workshop.

## Appendix C — Core language for safe facilitation

Use short, respectful phrases that preserve autonomy.

> “Let’s pause before we click anything. Unexpected urgency is a reason to check, not a reason to rush.”

> “You never need to give anyone your password or verification code to get help with this workshop.”

> “I do not know the answer yet, and I do not want to guess. Let’s check a trusted source or ask the program supervisor.”

> “Would you like to try the next step while I talk it through?”

> “AI can be useful, but it can be wrong. Keep private information out of it and verify important answers.”
